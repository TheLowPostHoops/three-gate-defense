Load & Durability

Who is likely to miss games next season, and which of the usual suspects (heavy minutes, miles run, back-to-backs, a coach who rides his starters, college workload, past injuries) actually predict it. Every input had a pass/fail bar written down before it was tested.

The game asks for more running

Projected games missed,

Expected games missed out of 82, counting only absences of three or more games in a row, for every player with 1,000+ minutes last season. The range is where 80% of similar forecasts landed in the backtest. Click a player for his load, miles, college record and what moves his number.

Coaches: who rides his starters hardest

Coach load is how many minutes a night his five most-used players averaged, compared with the league that season, over every season he was a head coach since 2010-11. Rest is how many one- and two-game breaks his rotation players took per season. Coach load did not forecast injuries (test L3 below), so this is a description of style, not a risk score.

Teams,

Nobody publishes how teams train or rehab players, so these lines measure what the work produces. None of them repeats from one season to the next well enough to call it a lasting staff effect, except rest policy, which is a choice teams make on purpose.

The test record

Each line is a question, the bar it had to clear (written before the test was run), and what happened. Only inputs that clear their bar go into the projected number. The full rules are in LOAD_PREREG.txt, including the fixes made after a first run and the results before them.

One- and two-game breaks are left out because they are mostly rest. It includes injuries, illness, suspensions and personal leave, because play-by-play cannot tell them apart.

What this does not do

  • It does not know what the injury was. No public data gives the body part or type for every absence, so an ankle sprain and a torn ACL are both just games missed.
  • High school is not in it. There is no public record of high-school injuries or minutes. College is in it as workload and games missed (Bart Torvik's player files, 2008 on, matched by name), not as injuries.
  • It cannot see rest decisions. One- and two-game breaks are left out, but a longer planned shutdown still counts as missed games.
  • Heavy minutes look safe for a reason. Players who are already hurting play fewer minutes, so the healthiest players are the ones who log big loads. That hides some of any real effect of workload, and no public data can fully undo it.
  • Miles data has holes. Camera tracking starts in 2013-14. NBA.com's 2025-26 file had 44 players with impossible speeds (13 to 22 mph); those rows are dropped.
  • Teams are last season's. The team column is where each player finished 2025-26. Offseason moves are not reflected.
  • It is a forecast for groups, not a diagnosis. A 50% chance of missing 20 games means half of players like him did. It says nothing about any one player's body.
